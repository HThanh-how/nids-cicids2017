\begin{abstract}
Machine-learned intrusion detectors report near-perfect scores on public
benchmarks, and practitioners do not see that performance in the field. We
show that a large part of the discrepancy is produced by the evaluation
itself, and we measure how large. Holding the models, the hyperparameters and
the preprocessing fixed on CICIDS2017, and re-fitting feature selection,
oversampling and scaling inside every training split, we compare a
conventional stratified random split against a day-disjoint split in which no
attack family is shared between training and test. Attack recall for XGBoost
falls from 0.9935 to 0.3164 and macro-F1 from 0.9879 to 0.6887; Random Forest
behaves the same way. The ranking of models does not survive either: Logistic
Regression, the weakest detector under the random split, becomes the
strongest under the day-disjoint one, so a benchmark that flatters a model can
also select the wrong one. Controls that separate near-duplicate leakage,
temporal shift and unseen attack families attribute the gap almost entirely
to unseen families, and the inversion to a less selective default threshold
rather than better generalisation. Repeating the conventional protocol over five
seeds further shows that the usual claim of a best model is unsupported --
XGBoost leads Random Forest by 0.00044 macro-F1, which neither a paired
$t$-test nor a Wilcoxon signed-rank test separates from zero. We then
translate the measured error rates into precision at operational attack
prevalence, where benchmark precision does not transfer, and we measure the model served as a
containerised endpoint under controlled load: it sustains 404~requests/s
before latency collapses, where the amortised per-sample model cost -- the
figure the literature, and an earlier version of this work, mistakes for a
service throughput -- would predict $9.5\times10^{5}$~flows/s, an
overstatement by a factor of roughly 2{,}300 that a per-stage breakdown
traces to scoring one flow per request. Code, manifests and raw results are
released.
\keywords{Intrusion detection \and Evaluation methodology \and Data leakage
\and CICIDS2017 \and Model serving \and Network security.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Learning-based intrusion detection reports macro-F1 above 0.98 as a matter
of routine on public benchmarks, and practitioners do not observe it in the
field. The discrepancy is usually attributed to concept drift or to the age
of the corpora. We examine a more immediate contributor: much of the reported
accuracy is an artefact of how the benchmark is split and of what the metric
measures.

A random split scatters the flows of every attack episode, and of every
family, across the train/test boundary, so a model is tested on kinds of
traffic it has already seen, often on near-duplicates of training flows. That
this flatters intrusion detectors has been observed
before~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers,xu2025robust}; we do not claim the
observation but contribute a protocol that removes the confound, controls
that say which part of it matters, and a measurement of what it costs.
Separately, precision depends on prevalence, and benchmarks carry orders of
magnitude more attack traffic than a production link; and deployment claims
routinely rest on an \emph{amortised per-sample model cost}, which is not a
property of a service -- an earlier version of this work converted such a
figure into a throughput, which prompted this study.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Holding models, hyperparameters and preprocessing fixed and varying only the
evaluation, we contribute: a pipeline that re-fits scaling, selection and
oversampling \emph{inside} every training split (Section~\ref{sec:leakage});
four splitting protocols on identical data -- repeated holdout, day-disjoint
(here also family-disjoint), leave-one-family-out and cross-dataset transfer
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) -- with controls that separate near-duplicate
leakage, temporal shift and unseen families (Section~\ref{sec:decomp});
paired tests showing the usual ranking of the leading models to be
unsupported (Section~\ref{sec:paired}); precision and alert burden at
operational prevalence (Section~\ref{sec:operational}); and an end-to-end,
per-stage measurement of the deployed service, including replication and
failure recovery (Section~\ref{sec:serving}). Code, deployment definitions
and raw results, from which every table and figure is generated, are
released.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/HThanh-how/nids-cicids2017}}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Buczak and Guven~\cite{buczak2016survey} survey the method families; our
concern is evaluation rather than models.

NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nslkdd} corrected redundant records in KDD'99,
UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015unswnb15} introduced a contemporary feature set,
and CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} added five days of labelled
traffic across several attack families. CICIDS2017 is not a neutral
instrument: Engelen et al.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting} document
labelling and capture defects -- misordered and duplicated packets, executed
but unlabelled attacks -- and Lanvin et al.~\cite{lanvin2023errors} show that
correcting them changes measured performance materially. We therefore report
differences between protocols on identical data rather than absolute scores.

That the evaluation of learned intrusion detectors is the weak link is an
old observation. Axelsson~\cite{axelsson1999baserate} showed that at
realistic attack prevalence the false-positive rate, not the detection rate,
bounds usefulness; Sommer and Paxson~\cite{sommer2010closedworld} argued
that closed-world benchmarks systematically overstate what machine learning
delivers on live traffic; Arp et al.~\cite{arp2023lessons} catalogue the
recurring pitfalls -- sampling bias, data snooping, base-rate neglect -- and
Kapoor and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023leakage} document leakage as a general
cause of irreproducible machine-learning results. That random splits flatter
intrusion detectors in particular is likewise not new, and we do not claim
it. Moczkodan and Ragab~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers} compare random,
temporal and five-tuple-grouped splits on this corpus and find the random
convention substantially overestimates performance; Xu and
Liu~\cite{xu2025robust} report severe recall loss on novel attacks. Models
learned on CICIDS2017 do not carry to related
corpora~\cite{dhooge2020interdataset,catillo2022transferability}, cross-domain
generalisation is absent and asymmetric~\cite{layeghy2023crossdomain}, part of
the problem is that each corpus has its own feature
extractor~\cite{sarhan2022standard}, and evaluation should be judged by
operational value~\cite{apruzzese2023sok}.

Against this work, what is new here is specific. (i) A day-disjoint split
that on this corpus is necessarily family-disjoint, decomposed by controls
into near-duplicate exposure, temporal shift on benign traffic and unseen
families (Section~\ref{sec:decomp}), where the prior studies report one
grouped or temporal split. (ii) The consequence for \emph{model selection}:
over repeated seeds the ranking of four models inverts between protocols, and
a matched-alert-rate analysis shows the inversion to be a threshold effect
rather than better generalisation. (iii) An oracle recalibration control
that separates threshold failure from ranking failure in cross-dataset
transfer. (iv) A translation of error rates into precision at
stated prevalences, and (v) a measurement of serving cost, broken down by
stage, where the literature offers an amortised per-sample model cost --
typically of order $10^{-2}$~ms -- as evidence of deployability.
Recent applied studies~\cite{talukder2024ml,almuhanna2025dl} confirm the
benchmark's continued use, mostly under a random split.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}
CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} holds five working days of labelled
flows -- benign traffic plus denial-of-service, DDoS, brute-force,
web-attack, botnet, port-scan and infiltration families -- each described by
more than eighty flow statistics, in eight CSV files, one per capture period.
We draw a fixed 20\,\% stratified sample from each file, which preserves
every family's share and lets the full protocol complete on one workstation
(the fraction is a parameter of the released driver; a full-corpus check is in
Section~\ref{sec:p2}). After normalising column names, replacing infinities,
dropping incomplete records and removing exact duplicates, the working set
holds 535{,}069 flows with 78 numeric features and an attack ratio of
0.1769.

\paragraph{Family partition.} The sample carries fourteen attack labels, and
each occurs on exactly one capture day: Tuesday FTP- and SSH-Patator;
Wednesday the four DoS variants (Hulk, GoldenEye, slowloris, Slowhttptest)
and Heartbleed; Thursday the three web attacks (brute force, XSS, SQL
injection) and infiltration; Friday botnet, port scan and DDoS. We treat each
label as a family. Thirteen appear in the random-split test set of
Figure~\ref{fig:family}; Heartbleed, with three flows, has none there.
Leave-one-family-out needs at least 50 flows to hold out, which leaves eleven
families; Infiltration (7), SQL injection (5) and Heartbleed (3) are too
small and are reported only within the aggregate.

Each flow keeps its \emph{capture day}, recovered from its source file, and
its \emph{original multi-class label}: the first makes the day-disjoint split
possible, the second the per-family analysis. The binary target maps every
non-benign label to the positive class. Standardisation, top-$k$ selection by
Random Forest importance~\cite{breiman2001random} and oversampling with
SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote} are treated as part of the model, not of the
data (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:method}
The models are deliberately ordinary; what varies is the protocol.

\subsection{Models, Hyperparameters and Leakage Control}
\label{sec:leakage}
We compare Logistic Regression, Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001random},
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and a two-hidden-layer perceptron, with every
hyperparameter fixed a priori and identical across every split, seed and
dataset; we perform no tuning, so no model is advantaged by a larger search
budget and nothing can have been selected on test data. Logistic Regression:
$\ell_2$, $C=1$, \texttt{lbfgs}, 1000 iterations. Random Forest: 200 trees,
unlimited depth, $\sqrt{d}$ features per split, Gini. XGBoost: 300 rounds,
depth 8, learning rate 0.3, no subsampling, $\lambda=1$, \texttt{hist}.
Perceptron: layers $(128,64)$, ReLU, Adam, $\alpha=10^{-4}$, batch 200,
learning rate $10^{-3}$, 50 epochs with early stopping. The machine-readable
specification is released with the artefact.

Preprocessing fitted before the split leaks test information into training,
and feature selection is the worst offender. For every split in every
protocol, therefore, the scaler is fitted on the training portion only, the
Random-Forest ranking selecting the top $k=20$ features is computed on the
scaled training portion only, and SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote} is applied to
the training portion only, after selection. The selected set is thus a random
variable; its stability is reported in Section~\ref{sec:featstab}.

\subsection{Four Splitting Protocols}
The same pipeline is evaluated under four separations of training from test;
only the last two resemble what a deployed sensor faces.
\textbf{(P1) Repeated stratified holdout} -- a 70/30 split over five seeds,
the conventional protocol and our reference point.
\textbf{(P2) Day-disjoint} -- training on Monday--Wednesday, testing on
Thursday--Friday, which removes the near-duplicate flows a random split
scatters across the boundary. Each attack family on CICIDS2017 was executed
on one day, so this split is also \emph{family-disjoint}: every attack at
test time is of a type absent from training.
\textbf{(P3) Leave-one-family-out} -- one family removed from training
entirely and the model asked to flag it, separating the family boundary from
the day boundary.
\textbf{(P4) Cross-dataset transfer} -- fitted on one corpus, evaluated on
the other with no target-domain data, on an explicit feature alignment
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). We distinguish this from
\emph{independent-dataset replication}, re-running the protocol within
UNSW-NB15; the two are frequently conflated, including in an earlier version
of this work, and only P4 speaks to cross-domain behaviour.

\subsection{Statistical Treatment and Metrics}
\label{sec:stats}
Every repeatable protocol is run over multiple seeds with all models on
identical splits, so comparisons are paired; we report means with 95\,\%
confidence half-widths, and for the leading pair a paired $t$-test and a
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Alongside accuracy, precision, recall and
macro-F1 we report false-positive and false-negative rates, MCC, balanced
accuracy, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC, the last being the informative one under heavy
imbalance. Precision depends on prevalence while TPR and FPR do not, so a
benchmark precision does not transfer to deployment; for prevalence $\pi$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ppv}
\mathrm{PPV}(\pi)=\frac{\pi\cdot\mathrm{TPR}}
{\pi\cdot\mathrm{TPR}+(1-\pi)\cdot\mathrm{FPR}},
\end{equation}
evaluated at $\pi \in \{10^{-1},\dots,10^{-4}\}$ with the implied false
alerts per million flows.

\subsection{Is the Leading Model Actually Leading?}
\label{sec:paired}
The usual conclusion from a table like this is that XGBoost wins. XGBoost
does lead Random Forest, but by 0.00044 macro-F1, which the paired $t$-test
does not separate from zero ($p=0.13$); the Wilcoxon signed-rank test agrees
($p=0.19$), though with five seeds its smallest attainable two-sided $p$ is
0.0625. Not detecting a difference is not showing equivalence, and five seeds
give little power; the honest summary is the 95\,\% interval of XGBoost's
paired advantage, $-0.0002$ to $0.0011$, which bounds any difference at about
a thousandth of macro-F1 but cannot resolve a smaller one. Both ensembles are
separated from Logistic Regression and the MLP by large, clearly significant
margins. We therefore do not name a best model: the data supports two tiers,
with the choice inside the upper one resting on other grounds
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}).

\subsection{P2: Day-Disjoint, and the Optimism of a Random Split}
\label{sec:p2}
Training uses Monday--Wednesday and testing Thursday--Friday; training holds
the denial-of-service variants, both brute-force families and Heartbleed,
testing holds botnet, DDoS, infiltration, port scanning and the three
web-attack variants. Attack recall for XGBoost falls from 0.9935 to 0.3164
and for Random Forest from 0.9937 to 0.3109, macro-F1 by roughly 0.30 for
both: models that find essentially every attack under the conventional
protocol miss two in three under this one (Table~\ref{tab:protocols}). This is
not an artefact of the sample: re-running both protocols on the full corpus
of 2{,}563{,}107 flows over three seeds gives XGBoost recall 0.9946 under the
random split and 0.3734 under the day-disjoint one, with Logistic Regression
again first at macro-F1 0.7882 against 0.7294.
Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows where the errors go. The false-positive rate \emph{improves}, from 0.0073 to
0.0030, while false negatives rise from 185 flows to 36{,}506: the detector
does not become noisy, it becomes quiet, confident and wrong -- the failure
a dashboard is least likely to reveal.

Nor does the ranking survive. Logistic Regression is last under the random
split (macro-F1 0.8515 against 0.9879) and first under the day-disjoint one
(0.7433 against 0.6887), with attack recall 0.4975 against 0.3164. The gap is
largest for the models that score best, so the conventional protocol can
recommend the wrong model; Section~\ref{sec:operational} qualifies what that
inversion does and does not imply. The day boundary removes near-duplicate
flows and attack families at once; the next section separates them.

\subsection{Near-Duplicates, Temporal Shift or Unseen Families?}
\label{sec:decomp}
The day boundary changes three things at once: it keeps near-duplicates off
opposite sides, moves the test to later days, and tests only on families
absent from training. Three controls separate them (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}).

\emph{Near-duplicates.} We call two flows near-duplicates when all 78
features agree to two significant figures. Under the random split 15.5\,\% of
test flows, and 21.4\,\% of test attacks, have such a twin in training; under
P2, 0.0075\,\% of test attacks do. A random split grouped so that no
near-duplicate group straddles the boundary removes that exposure entirely,
yet XGBoost keeps recall 0.9821 and macro-F1 0.9870, against 0.9935 and
0.9879: at this tolerance near-duplicates account for about a hundredth of
recall, not two thirds of it. \emph{Temporal shift without new families.}
Trained on 70\,\% of Monday--Wednesday, XGBoost has FPR 0.0030 on the other
30\,\% of those days and 0.0033 on the benign flows of Thursday--Friday
(Logistic Regression 0.0563 and 0.0540): benign traffic two days later is
no harder to recognise. \emph{Unseen families.} The same models detect
held-out attacks of the training days at 0.9975 and the Thursday--Friday
attacks at 0.3218. A matched-family cross-day split, the cleanest test of
temporal shift on attacks, does not exist here: no attack label spans two
days. On this corpus, then, the optimism of the random split is almost
entirely the unseen-family effect. Two qualifications: a looser tolerance, or
the flow-identity grouping this release cannot support
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), could attribute more to leakage; and any
temporal shift in attack traffic is folded into the family effect.

\emph{Generalisation or a looser boundary?} Re-running P2 over five seeds
with the scores kept (the three reported plus two) reproduces the inversion
-- Logistic Regression recall 0.4859 against 0.3135 for XGBoost, macro-F1
0.7397 against 0.6873 -- but at FPR 0.0564 against 0.0025. Threshold-free
the order reverses: ROC-AUC 0.6448 against 0.8526 for XGBoost and 0.8412 for
Random Forest (ensembles $\pm0.067$ over seeds). Held to Logistic
Regression's alert rate, Random Forest recalls 0.5949 and XGBoost 0.4750
against its 0.4860; held to XGBoost's, Logistic Regression recalls 0.2428.
Its advantage is a less selective decision boundary, concentrated in DDoS
(0.9615 against 0.6408 for XGBoost) and the web attacks, not a better
separation of unseen attacks; botnet and port scanning are missed by every
model. The inversion is real at default thresholds, which is how such models
are reported, and is a warning about those thresholds rather than evidence
that the linear model generalises.

\subsection{P3: Per-Family Behaviour and Unseen Attack Types}
\label{sec:p3}
Aggregate scores average over families weighted by flow count.
Figure~\ref{fig:family} breaks the random-split result down: a macro-F1 of
0.988 coexists with XGBoost detecting SQL injection at 0.000 and cross-site
scripting at 0.023 -- families too small to cost the aggregate anything. The
model that looks worst in aggregate is the only one that detects them:
Logistic Regression reaches 1.00 on XSS and 0.93 on web brute force where
the three stronger models sit between 0.02 and 0.09, by way of a
false-positive rate an order of magnitude higher.

\subsection{Feature Selection: Reproducible but Not Canonical}
\label{sec:featstab}
Across seeds the top-20 selection is stable (mean pairwise Jaccard 0.756; 15
of 20 chosen by every seed). Across criteria it is not: impurity and
permutation importance on the same forest agree on 4 of 20, mutual
information sides with impurity (0.75), and either list yields a similar
macro-F1, so a published top-$k$ list is a property of the criterion as much
as of the traffic.

\subsection{Independent Replication, and P4: Cross-Dataset Transfer}
\label{sec:transfer}
Re-running the whole protocol \emph{within} UNSW-NB15 gives XGBoost macro-F1
0.9504 and Random Forest 0.9489, so the two-tier ordering reproduces on
independent data. That is replication, not transfer: no model leaves its own
corpus.

Transfer fits on one corpus and evaluates on the other with no target-domain
data. The corpora share no schema and come from different extractors:
CICIDS2017 from CICFlowMeter~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids}, whose
bidirectional flows take the direction of their first packet as forward,
and UNSW-NB15 from Argus and Bro-IDS~\cite{moustafa2015unswnb15}, whose
records are oriented source to destination. We align the seven quantities
whose documented meaning matches -- duration (\texttt{Flow Duration} to
\texttt{dur}, microseconds to seconds), packets (\texttt{spkts},
\texttt{dpkts}), bytes (\texttt{sbytes}, \texttt{dbytes}) and mean packet
size (\texttt{smean}, \texttt{dmean}) in each direction, CICIDS2017's
forward and backward counterparts on the other side. Matching documentation is not matching definition:
each tool cuts traffic into flows by its own rules, and neither documents
its byte and size fields precisely enough to establish identical header
accounting. Part of any transfer failure may therefore be measurement
mismatch rather than a change of traffic, which this experiment cannot
separate; a common extractor~\cite{sarhan2022standard} would. The feature
restriction is a further confound, so we measure it: an
in-domain holdout on exactly these seven features gives XGBoost 0.9911 on
CICIDS2017 and 0.9289 on UNSW-NB15 against 0.9879 and 0.9504 on twenty, so
what follows is domain shift, not feature starvation.

Transfer fails in both directions, and differently in each. Fitted on
CICIDS2017 and tested on UNSW-NB15 the models call almost everything benign:
attack recall 0.0000 for Random Forest, 0.0001 for XGBoost, 0.0229 for the
MLP. Fitted on UNSW-NB15 and tested on CICIDS2017 they flag almost
everything: false-positive rates 0.55--0.89, precision 0.11--0.21 despite
recall up to 0.85. Macro-F1 lands between 0.24 and 0.42 against 0.99
in-domain. Each failure runs towards the majority decision of its source
corpus -- 17.7\,\% attack against 68.1\,\% -- which suggests a threshold
calibrated to the source prevalence. We test that cause with an oracle no
deployment has, re-thresholding on the target with its own labels
(Table~\ref{tab:recal}). The threshold is part of the failure: from
CICIDS2017 to UNSW-NB15 the best threshold lifts the MLP from 0.264 to 0.772
macro-F1 and Logistic Regression from 0.254 to 0.698. It is not all of it:
ROC-AUC, which no threshold changes, is 0.382 and 0.254 for Random Forest in
the two directions and 0.563 and 0.459 for XGBoost, at or below chance, so
for the tree ensembles the \emph{ranking} of flows does not transfer; only
the MLP stays above 0.7 both ways (0.778, 0.700). A flow-statistics
classifier trained on one public corpus thus does not carry to another, which
contradicts citing an in-domain score as evidence of general applicability;
labelled recalibration rescues the smoother models only in part, and domain
adaptation is untested here.

\subsection{What the Error Rates Mean in Operation}
\label{sec:operational}
Each test set so far has its own attack share: 17.7\,\% under the random
split, 24.2\,\% on the day-disjoint test days (13.1\,\% on its training
days), 68.1\,\% on UNSW-NB15, and 100\,\% in a leave-one-family-out fold,
which scores only the held-out family. TPR and FPR are conditional on the
class and so do not depend on that share; Equation~\ref{eq:ppv} carries them
to the prevalence of a production link (Table~\ref{tab:operational}). Each
entry is a conditional scenario -- the precision on a link at prevalence
$\pi$ \emph{if} the measured rates held there -- not a bound in either
direction, since Section~\ref{sec:transfer} shows that rates measured on one
corpus need not hold on another. All four models collapse, and
the collapse is governed by the false-positive rate -- Axelsson's base-rate
fallacy~\cite{axelsson1999baserate}, here with measured rates. The most favourable case
in this paper -- XGBoost, random split, recall 0.9935, FPR 0.0073 -- has
precision 0.120 at one attack in a thousand, seven of every eight alerts
false and 7{,}257 false alerts per million flows; at one in ten thousand,
0.013.

The comparison across protocols is easy to misread. Under the day-disjoint
protocol XGBoost's alert burden \emph{improves} -- 7{,}257 to 3{,}006 false
alerts per million, precision 0.120 to 0.095 -- while recall falls from
0.9935 to 0.3164: the degradation is invisible in exactly the signals a
deployment has. Nor does the inversion of Section~\ref{sec:p2} recommend
Logistic Regression: at one in a thousand its precision is 0.0083 with
59{,}143 false alerts per million, twenty times XGBoost's burden for a recall
advantage of 0.18. On UNSW-NB15, recall above 0.96 comes with FPR near
0.075, so precision at one in a thousand is 0.013 rather than 0.120 -- an
order of magnitude of operational value between two standard benchmarks.

\subsection{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:cost}
Timings are the mean of five repeats after a discarded warm-up, on an AMD
workstation with 16 logical cores and 27.8~GiB running Python 3.13, scikit-learn 1.5.2,
XGBoost 3.4.1 and imbalanced-learn 0.14.2. XGBoost trains in
$5.96 \pm 1.41$~s against $32.65 \pm 2.48$~s for Random Forest, a sixth of
the cost for a quality difference Section~\ref{sec:paired} could not
distinguish from zero. Amortised inference is 0.001056~ms per flow for XGBoost -- model cost
spread over a full batch on all cores, a lower bound on serving a flow, which
we do not convert into a throughput.

\section{Deployment and Measured Serving Cost}
\label{sec:serving}
An offline score says what a detector would decide, not what the system
around it costs to run; we measure the deployed service.

\subsection{The Service and the Load Protocol}
The evaluated XGBoost model and its standardisation statistics are wrapped
in a stateless FastAPI service (liveness, readiness, single-flow and batch
endpoints; every response carries its in-process scoring time), one worker
per replica in a pinned, non-root image. Load is closed-loop -- each of $N$
clients issues its next request as the previous returns, so saturation
cannot hide behind an unbounded queue -- with a discarded warm-up and
held-out CICIDS2017 flows as payloads.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:servingresults}
At the library's default thread setting a single replica sustains
309~requests/s at eight clients (median 24~ms, p95 39~ms), then collapses:
188~requests/s at 32 clients with p95 578~ms, 128~requests/s at 128 clients
with p99 6.2~s, no request failing at any level. The amortised model cost
of Section~\ref{sec:cost} inverts to about $9.5\times10^{5}$~flows/s; the
best configuration we measured sustains 404~requests/s
(Section~\ref{sec:replication}), an overstatement by a factor of roughly
2{,}300 -- the basis on which an earlier version of this work quoted
$10^{5}$~predictions/s. Batching recovers much of it: a batch of 256 reaches
34{,}662~flows/s, still 27 times below the extrapolation, at p95 88~ms -- 114
times the flow throughput for twice the median request latency. Process
launch to first successful readiness takes 7.83~s, of which 1.95~s is
loading and warming the model.

\paragraph{Where the factor comes from.} Timing each stage of a single-flow
request at one closed-loop client, with one maths thread, gives an end-to-end
mean of 2.93~ms: 1.04~ms in standardisation and the model call, 0.81~ms in the
web framework (routing, parsing and validation, serialisation, middleware)
and 1.08~ms in transport, the ASGI server and the co-located client. Timed in
process, parsing and validating the JSON body and serialising the reply cost
only 0.02 and 0.01~ms, so the framework share is dispatch rather than
encoding. The model call on one row takes 1.03~ms at the median against
0.0070~ms per row when the same call scores 2{,}000 rows, about 150 times.
The 2{,}300-fold gap is therefore architectural: one flow per request pays
the model library's fixed per-call cost and the HTTP stack once per flow,
neither of which appears in an amortised figure, and batching amortises both.
At the default thread setting every share is larger (6.41~ms end to end).

\subsection{Replication, Failure and the Resource Envelope}
\label{sec:replication}
We run $N$ service processes, round-robin the load at eight clients per
replica, kill one under load, and record CPU and memory throughout.

Each request scores one 20-feature row, and by default the numerical
libraries fan that across every core, so replicas contend: throughput falls
from 269 to 88~requests/s as replicas go from one to eight. Pinning each
replica to one maths thread -- what the image sets, and what we had
previously asserted without evidence -- is worth 1.5 to 2.8 times at every
replica count, and 404 against 269~requests/s even for a single replica,
where no inter-replica contention exists. Past two replicas the pinned
throughput also declines, but the load generator shares the sixteen logical
cores, so that portion is bounded by client contention.

Killing one of four replicas under load, three times, lost exactly 35
requests of roughly 8{,}000 each time; the replica served again after 3.13~s
and throughput returned to baseline 8~s after the kill; a Kubernetes Service
would also deregister the dead endpoint, so 35 is an upper bound. Resident
memory is 176~MiB per replica pinned, 189--196~MiB unpinned, flat in
replica count; our Deployment had requested 512~MiB and limited at 1~GiB,
chosen before any measurement, and now derives both from it. What still
needs a cluster -- image pull, pod placement and the autoscaler's control-loop
delay -- is not measured and not estimated.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
CICIDS2017 carries documented labelling and capture
defects~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting,lanvin2023errors}, so absolute
scores are not field accuracy; differences between protocols on identical
data are more robust, but their size on a corrected or modern corpus is not
established. The release we use has no addresses, source ports or
timestamps, so the five-tuple or time grouping
of~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers} cannot be rebuilt; our near-duplicate
control groups on feature values, at one tolerance. Because every family
occurs on one day, temporal shift is measured on benign traffic only.
Leave-one-family-out uses one model and one seed, and five random-split and
three day-disjoint seeds (five in the threshold analysis) give little power:
an undetected difference is bounded by its interval, not shown to be zero.
We cover two corpora and four conventional models with hyperparameters fixed
a priori, and no deep sequence or Transformer detector; whether such models
narrow the family gap is open, and the protocol applies to them unchanged.
Serving was measured on one host under closed-loop load, with the load
generator co-located and one software stack (FastAPI, JSON over HTTP/1.1);
open-loop arrivals, network hops, cluster scheduling and other stacks, which
would shift the framework and transport shares, are not measured.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On CICIDS2017, with everything but the split held fixed, most of a benchmark
score belongs to the protocol: tree-ensemble attack recall falls from about
0.99 to about 0.31 once no attack family is shared between training and test
-- because the test families are unseen, not because of near-duplicates or
benign drift -- and the ranking inverts at default thresholds. Benchmark
precision does not survive a production prevalence, and the degradation is
invisible to an operator watching alert volume. The served endpoint sustains
404~requests/s where its amortised model cost predicts
$9.5\times10^{5}$~flows/s, because one flow per request pays the model's
per-call cost and the HTTP stack each time. None of this says learned
intrusion detection does not work; it says the published numbers answer an
easier question than the operational one.

\paragraph{Guidelines for evaluating a flow-based NIDS.} As a checklist:
\textbf{(G1)} report a split that shares no attack episode between training
and test -- day-, family- or flow-grouped -- beside the random one, and
report the gap, not only the better number;
\textbf{(G2)} fit scaling, feature selection and oversampling inside each
training split;
\textbf{(G3)} report per-family recall with the family's test count, and
leave-one-family-out for every family large enough to hold out;
\textbf{(G4)} repeat over seeds, report intervals and paired tests, and do
not read a non-significant difference as equivalence;
\textbf{(G5)} rank detectors at a stated operating point or with
threshold-free metrics, since a default threshold can invert a ranking;
\textbf{(G6)} state the attack prevalence of every test set and translate
error rates to the prevalence of the intended link, as a conditional
scenario;
\textbf{(G7)} for cross-dataset claims, document the feature alignment and
separate threshold from ranking failure; and
\textbf{(G8)} measure the served endpoint end to end, by stage, and never
convert an amortised model cost into a throughput.
Next: the protocol on a corrected~\cite{lanvin2023errors} and a contemporary
corpus; deep sequence models and one-class detectors under the same
day-disjoint split, where they may overturn the ordering here; and the
cluster-level serving measurements.
