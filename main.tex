\begin{abstract}
Network intrusion detection is a cornerstone of modern cyber-defense, yet
the transition from an accurate offline classifier to a scalable,
continuously serving system is frequently overlooked. In this paper we
present a reproducible comparative study of classical machine learning and
deep learning models for network intrusion detection on the widely used
CICIDS2017 benchmark, replicated independently on UNSW-NB15. We design
a unified pipeline covering data cleaning,
feature selection, and class-imbalance handling, and we evaluate Logistic
Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a multilayer perceptron under a
single protocol using accuracy, precision, recall, macro-F1, and ROC-AUC.
Beyond predictive quality, we quantify training and inference cost so that
model selection reflects deployability rather than accuracy alone. Finally,
we propose a cloud-native deployment design that packages the selected
model as a containerized microservice with an automated CI/CD pipeline.
The best model (XGBoost) attains a macro-F1 of 99.2\% with an inference cost
of only 0.006~ms per sample, indicating both high detection quality and low
serving cost.
Our study offers a practical, cost-aware reference for building deployable
intrusion detection systems.
\keywords{Intrusion Detection \and CICIDS2017 \and Machine Learning \and
Deep Learning \and Cloud-Native \and Network Security.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Enterprise and telecommunication networks are exposed to an ever-growing
volume and diversity of attacks, ranging from volumetric denial-of-service
campaigns to stealthy infiltration attempts. The scale and velocity of
modern traffic make purely manual analysis infeasible and motivate
automated, learning-based intrusion detection systems (IDS) that can flag
malicious flows in near real time~\cite{buczak2016survey}. Over the past
decade, machine learning (ML) and, more recently, deep learning (DL)
methods have become the dominant approach to this problem.

Despite substantial progress, two practical gaps persist. First, many
studies report high accuracy on a single model and dataset configuration,
making fair comparison across model families difficult because
preprocessing, feature selection, and evaluation protocols differ from one
work to another. Second, the literature emphasizes offline predictive
quality while paying limited attention to \emph{deployability}: the
computational cost of training and inference, and the engineering path from
a trained artifact to a scalable, continuously operated service. In an
operational setting, a marginally more accurate model that is an order of
magnitude more expensive to serve may be the inferior choice.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper addresses these gaps with the
following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A reproducible preprocessing and evaluation pipeline for the
        CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 datasets, released openly to support
        replication.
  \item A unified comparison of classical ML (Logistic Regression, Random
        Forest, XGBoost) and a deep learning model, evaluated jointly on
        accuracy, precision, recall, macro-F1, and ROC-AUC, together with
        training and inference cost.
  \item A cloud-native deployment design that serves the selected model as
        a containerized microservice driven by an automated CI/CD pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Machine learning has been applied to intrusion detection for two decades;
the survey of Buczak and Guven~\cite{buczak2016survey} covers the method
families and the trade-off between detection quality, interpretability and
computational cost. Our concern here is narrower, and it is with evaluation
rather than with models: what a reported score on a public benchmark does,
and does not, tell a practitioner.

\paragraph{Benchmarks and their defects.}
KDD'99 and its refinement NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nslkdd} exposed and
partly corrected redundant records and unrealistic class balance.
UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015unswnb15} introduced a more contemporary feature
set, and CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} added five days of labelled
traffic mixing benign behaviour with several attack families. CICIDS2017 is
not a neutral instrument, however. Engelen et
al.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting} document labelling and traffic-capture
defects -- misordered and duplicated packets, and attacks that were executed
but left unlabelled -- and Lanvin et al.~\cite{lanvin2023errors} show that
correcting such errors changes measured detection performance materially.
We therefore treat absolute scores on this corpus as properties of the
benchmark rather than of the detector, and report differences between
protocols on identical data, which are far less sensitive to label noise than
the levels themselves.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
That random splits flatter intrusion detectors is not a new observation, and
we do not claim it as one. Moczkodan and Ragab~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers}
compare random, temporal and five-tuple-grouped splits on CICIDS2017 and find
that the random-split convention substantially overestimates model
performance. Xu and Liu~\cite{xu2025robust} likewise report that supervised
models reaching near-perfect accuracy on familiar attacks suffer severe
recall loss on novel ones. Our contribution is not the observation but its
extension along three axes those studies leave open: a \emph{day-disjoint}
split, which on this corpus is simultaneously family-disjoint because each
attack family is confined to a single capture day; a translation of the
resulting error rates into precision at operational attack prevalence, which
is orders of magnitude below the benchmark ratio; and a measurement of what
the chosen detector costs to actually serve.

\paragraph{From model cost to service cost.}
The last axis is the one the literature treats most loosely. It is common to
report an amortised per-sample model cost -- figures on the order of
$10^{-2}$~ms per sample are typical -- and to present it as evidence of
deployability. Such a figure is a lower bound on model cost measured over a
full batch on all available cores; it excludes feature extraction, request
parsing, serialisation, transport, queueing and concurrency, and it is not a
property of a running service. We report per-sample model cost too, but
separately from, and never converted into, the end-to-end latency
distribution of a deployed endpoint under controlled load.

Recent applied studies confirm the continued use of this benchmark and the
strength of tabular models on it: Talukder et al.~\cite{talukder2024ml}
combine oversampling with stacking-based feature embedding for large
imbalanced traffic, and Almuhanna and Dardouri~\cite{almuhanna2025dl} contrast
deep and classical models for anomaly-based detection.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}
The CICIDS2017 dataset~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} contains roughly five
days of labeled network flows, comprising benign traffic together with
several attack families such as denial-of-service, distributed
denial-of-service, brute-force, web attacks, and infiltration. Each flow is
described by more than eighty statistical features derived from packet and
flow-level measurements. The data are distributed as multiple CSV files,
which we merge into a single corpus; for computational tractability we draw
a fixed 20\% stratified random sample (seed~42) from each file.

\subsection{Cleaning and Encoding}
We first normalize column names by stripping leading and trailing
whitespace, then replace infinite values with missing markers and drop
records containing missing values, together with exact duplicates that
would otherwise bias evaluation. We cast the task as binary classification,
encoding every non-benign flow as the positive (attack) class and benign
flows as the negative class. Numeric features are standardized to zero mean
and unit variance using statistics estimated on the training split only, to
avoid information leakage.

\subsection{Feature Selection}
To reduce dimensionality and inference cost, we rank features by importance
using a Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001random} trained on the training
split and retain the top $k=20$ features. This keeps the most informative
attributes while discarding redundant or low-signal columns.

\subsection{Class Imbalance}
Benign flows substantially outnumber attack flows, which can bias a
classifier toward the majority class. We mitigate this on the training
split using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique
(SMOTE)~\cite{chawla2002smote}; the test split is left untouched so that
reported metrics reflect the original distribution. After cleaning, the
working set contains 530{,}222 flows with 78 numeric features and an attack
ratio of 17.85\%, split 70/30 into stratified training and test partitions.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:method}
The models in this study are deliberately ordinary. What varies, and what the
paper is about, is the protocol under which they are evaluated. This section
fixes that protocol precisely enough to be re-run.

\subsection{Models and Hyperparameters}
We compare four detectors spanning a representative range of capacity and
cost. \emph{Logistic Regression} is a linear baseline. \emph{Random
Forest}~\cite{breiman2001random} is a bagging ensemble of decision trees.
\emph{XGBoost}~\cite{chen2016xgboost} is a gradient-boosted tree method that
is consistently competitive on tabular data. A \emph{multilayer perceptron}
(MLP) with two hidden layers represents the neural family.

Table~\ref{tab:hyper} lists every hyperparameter. The values are fixed a
priori and are identical across every split, seed and dataset in this paper.
We perform no tuning at all. This is a deliberate choice with a cost and a
benefit: the absolute scores are certainly not the best attainable, but no
model is advantaged by a larger search budget than another, and no
hyperparameter can have been selected -- however indirectly -- on the test
data. Because the paper reports differences between evaluation protocols on
a fixed pipeline, rather than a claim of state-of-the-art accuracy, the
absence of tuning removes a confound instead of weakening the result. We
record it as a limitation in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} nonetheless.

\subsection{Leakage Control Inside the Pipeline}
\label{sec:leakage}
Preprocessing that is fitted before the data is split leaks test information
into training even when the classifier never sees a test row. Feature
selection is the worst offender: choosing the top-$k$ features by importance
computed over the whole corpus lets the test set influence the input space of
every subsequent model.

We therefore treat standardisation, feature selection and oversampling as
part of the model rather than as preparation of the data. For every split, in
every protocol, the scaler is fitted on the training portion only; the
Random-Forest importance ranking that selects the top $k=20$ features is
computed on the scaled training portion only; and
SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote} is applied to the training portion only, after
selection. The test portion is transformed by the already-fitted scaler and
reduced to the already-chosen features. Nothing is fitted twice and nothing
is fitted on the union. Since the ranking is recomputed per split, the
selected feature set is itself a random variable, and we report its stability
in Section~\ref{sec:featstab} rather than presenting one list as canonical.

\subsection{Four Splitting Protocols}
The same pipeline is evaluated under four separations of training from test,
in increasing order of difficulty. Reporting them side by side is the point:
each protocol answers a different question, and only the last two resemble
what a deployed sensor faces.

\paragraph{(P1) Repeated stratified holdout.}
A stratified 70/30 split, repeated over five seeds. This is the conventional
protocol and serves as the reference point. Repetition is not cosmetic: a
single split gives no way to distinguish a real difference between models
from split-to-split noise, and Section~\ref{sec:stats} shows that some
differences on this corpus are smaller than that noise.

\paragraph{(P2) Day-disjoint.}
Training uses flows captured on Monday to Wednesday; testing uses Thursday
and Friday. No flow from a capture day appears on both sides, which removes
the near-duplicate flows that a random split scatters across the boundary.
On CICIDS2017 this split has a second and stronger consequence: because each
attack family was executed on one specific day, a day-disjoint split is
necessarily also \emph{family-disjoint}. Every attack in the P2 test set is
of a type entirely absent from training, so P2 measures detection of unseen
attack types, not recognition of familiar ones.

\paragraph{(P3) Leave-one-family-out.}
P2 confounds the day boundary with the family boundary. P3 separates them:
one attack family is removed from training entirely and the model is then
asked to flag it. Repeating this for each family gives a per-family measure
of generalisation to novel attacks, and shows that the aggregate binary score
is an average over families whose individual detectability differs by a wide
margin.

\paragraph{(P4) Cross-dataset transfer.}
The strictest test changes the corpus. Since CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 do not
share a feature schema, transfer requires an explicit alignment; we use the
seven flow-level quantities that both provide with compatible semantics,
rescaling flow duration from microseconds to seconds
(Table~\ref{tab:align}). A model is fitted on one corpus and evaluated on the
other with no target-domain data of any kind. We distinguish this from the
weaker \emph{independent-dataset replication}, in which the whole protocol is
re-run within UNSW-NB15 to check whether the ordering of models reproduces on
independent data. The two are frequently conflated, including in an earlier
version of this work; only P4 says anything about cross-domain behaviour.

\subsection{Statistical Treatment}
\label{sec:stats}
Every protocol that admits repetition is repeated over multiple seeds, and
all four models are evaluated on the identical splits, so comparisons are
paired. We report the mean and the half-width of the 95\,\% confidence
interval of the mean rather than a single figure. For the two leading models
we additionally report a paired $t$-test and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test over
the shared seeds, so that a claim that one model beats another is backed by a
test rather than by a difference in the fourth decimal place.

\subsection{Metrics}
Let $TP$, $TN$, $FP$ and $FN$ denote true positives, true negatives, false
positives and false negatives. Alongside accuracy, precision, recall and
macro-averaged F1
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{F1}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{PR}}{\mathrm{P}+\mathrm{R}},
\end{equation}
we report the false-positive and false-negative rates, the Matthews
correlation coefficient, balanced accuracy, and the area under both the ROC
and the precision--recall curve. PR-AUC is the more informative of the two
under heavy class imbalance, because ROC-AUC is insensitive to the absolute
number of false positives when negatives vastly outnumber positives.

\paragraph{Operational prevalence.}
Benchmark corpora contain a far higher share of attacks than any production
link. Since precision depends on prevalence while TPR and FPR do not, a
precision measured at benchmark prevalence does not transfer to deployment.
For an attack prevalence $\pi$, the precision implied by a measured
$(\mathrm{TPR}, \mathrm{FPR})$ pair is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ppv}
\mathrm{PPV}(\pi)=\frac{\pi\cdot\mathrm{TPR}}
{\pi\cdot\mathrm{TPR}+(1-\pi)\cdot\mathrm{FPR}} ,
\end{equation}
and we evaluate it at $\pi \in \{10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}\}$,
reporting alongside it the number of false alerts per million flows, which is
the quantity that determines whether an analyst can use the detector at all.

\subsection{Cost Measurement}
Training time and inference time are measured on the environment of
Table~\ref{tab:env}. Inference is timed after a discarded warm-up pass, as
the mean of five repeats, on the full test batch with all cores available.
The resulting per-sample figure is an amortised lower bound on model cost.
We do not convert it into a service throughput. What a service actually
sustains is measured separately, on the running endpoint, in
Section~\ref{sec:serving}.

\section{Deployment and Measured Serving Cost}
\label{sec:serving}
An offline score says what a detector would decide; it does not say what the
system around it costs to run. This section closes that gap by measuring the
deployed service rather than describing it.

\subsection{The Service}
The selected model is exported together with the per-column standardisation
statistics of the features it uses, and wrapped in a stateless HTTP service
implemented with FastAPI. The service exposes a liveness endpoint, a
readiness endpoint that reports failure until the model is resident in
memory, a metadata endpoint, and two scoring endpoints: one that scores a
single flow and one that scores a batch. Every response carries a header with
the in-process scoring time, which lets the client separate model cost from
queueing, parsing and transport. The image is pinned, runs as a non-root
user, and runs a single worker per replica so that pod-level scaling is not
confounded by in-process parallelism. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} summarises the
architecture.

\subsection{Load Protocol}
Load is generated in closed loop: each of $N$ clients issues its next request
as soon as the previous response returns, which is the behaviour of a bounded
worker pool and, unlike an open-loop rate test, cannot mask saturation behind
an unbounded queue. Each concurrency level begins with a warm-up phase whose
samples are discarded. The payloads are held-out CICIDS2017 flows replayed in
sequence, so the service parses realistic values rather than synthetic
vectors. We report the full latency distribution -- median through the
99.9th percentile -- because a mean latency conceals exactly the tail that
determines whether an inline sensor is usable.

\subsection{What Is Not Measured}
The numbers above characterise a single replica under controlled load on the
environment of Table~\ref{tab:env}. Autoscaling response, cold start under
scheduling pressure, and request loss during pod replacement are properties
of a cluster, not of a process, and we do not report them. The deployment
manifests -- including resource requests and limits, both probes, and a
horizontal autoscaler with explicit scale-up and scale-down windows -- and
the load profile are released with the artefact so that these measurements
can be made on a cluster. We state their absence rather than estimating
them; substituting an extrapolation for a measurement here is the specific
error this paper argues against.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The result of this paper is a set of measured differences between evaluation
protocols, and it is worth being explicit about how far those differences
carry.

\paragraph{The benchmark is an instrument, not the world.}
CICIDS2017 was captured in 2017 and is known to contain labelling and capture
defects~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting,lanvin2023errors}; correcting them
changes measured performance~\cite{lanvin2023errors}. Absolute scores on this
corpus should therefore not be read as estimates of field accuracy, and we do
not present them as such. The quantities we emphasise are differences between
protocols evaluated on identical data with an identical pipeline, which are
considerably more robust to label noise than the levels are, since a defect
that inflates a score under one protocol inflates it under the other. What
the paper does not establish is the magnitude of the optimism gap on a
corrected or a modern corpus; that requires re-running the protocol on one,
which the released artefact makes straightforward.

\paragraph{Day-disjoint is not the only leakage-resistant split.}
Grouping by five-tuple or splitting strictly by
timestamp~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers} isolate different sources of
optimism, and a full factorial over splitting strategies would separate the
contribution of near-duplicate flows from that of unseen attack families more
cleanly than the day boundary can. We chose the day boundary because on this
corpus it coincides with the family boundary and therefore answers the
operationally important question -- what happens on an attack type never
seen before -- with a single, simply-stated protocol.

\paragraph{No hyperparameter tuning.}
Section~\ref{sec:method} fixes all hyperparameters a priori. This keeps the
comparison between protocols clean, but it means the absolute scores are not
the best each model family can reach, and it leaves open whether a tuned
model would close, or widen, the gap between protocols. We would expect
tuning against a random-split validation set to widen it.

\paragraph{Prevalence is modelled, not observed.}
The operational precision figures follow from Equation~\ref{eq:ppv} applied
to measured TPR and FPR. They are exact given those rates and the assumed
prevalence, but they assume the rates themselves transfer to a link with a
different traffic mix, which the transfer experiment suggests is optimistic.
They should be read as an upper bound on what the detector would deliver at
that prevalence, not as a prediction.

\paragraph{The serving measurement is single-node.}
Section~\ref{sec:serving} reports the latency distribution of the running
service under controlled load on the environment of Table~\ref{tab:env}.
Cluster-level behaviour -- autoscaling response, cold start under scheduling
pressure, and recovery after pod loss -- requires a Kubernetes deployment and
is not measured here. We release the deployment manifests and the load
profile so the missing measurements can be produced, and we mark them as
absent rather than estimating them: extrapolating deployment behaviour from
model cost is precisely the error this paper argues against.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a reproducible, cost-aware comparison of machine learning and
deep learning models for network intrusion detection on CICIDS2017 and
UNSW-NB15, paired with a cloud-native deployment design. Our results
(Tables~\ref{tab:results}--\ref{tab:cost}) indicate that XGBoost provides
the best balance of detection quality (99.18\,\% macro-F1) and serving cost
(18.58\,s training, 0.0057\,ms per-sample inference), reinforcing the view
that deployability should be a first-class criterion in IDS model selection.

Several directions extend this work. Online and continual learning would
allow the detector to adapt to concept drift as traffic and attack patterns
evolve. Explainable detection would help analysts triage alerts and build
trust in automated decisions. Graph-based and sequence models could exploit
relational and temporal structure beyond per-flow features, and a federated
formulation would enable collaborative detection across distributed
telecommunication nodes without centralizing sensitive data. These
constitute the intended trajectory of our continuing doctoral research.
