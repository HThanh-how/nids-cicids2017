\begin{abstract}
Machine-learned intrusion detectors report near-perfect scores on public
benchmarks, and practitioners do not see that performance in the field. We
show that a large part of the discrepancy is produced by the evaluation
itself, and we measure how large. Holding the models, the hyperparameters and
the preprocessing fixed on CICIDS2017, and re-fitting feature selection,
oversampling and scaling inside every training split, we compare a
conventional stratified random split against a day-disjoint split in which no
attack family is shared between training and test. Attack recall for XGBoost
falls from 0.9935 to 0.3164 and macro-F1 from 0.9879 to 0.6887; Random Forest
behaves the same way. The ranking of models does not survive either: Logistic
Regression, the weakest detector under the random split, becomes the
strongest under the day-disjoint one, so a benchmark that flatters a model can
also select the wrong one. Repeating the conventional protocol over five
seeds further shows that the usual claim of a best model is unsupported --
XGBoost leads Random Forest by 0.00044 macro-F1, which neither a paired
$t$-test nor a Wilcoxon signed-rank test separates from zero. We then
translate the measured error rates into precision at operational attack
prevalence, where benchmark precision does not transfer, and we measure the model served as a
containerised endpoint under controlled load: it sustains 404~requests/s
before latency collapses, where the amortised per-sample model cost -- the
figure the literature, and an earlier version of this work, mistakes for a
service throughput -- would predict $9.5\times10^{5}$~flows/s, an
overstatement by a factor of 2{,}300. The experiment
driver, the serving implementation, the deployment manifests and the raw
result files from which every table and figure here is generated are released
with the paper.
\keywords{Intrusion detection \and Evaluation methodology \and Data leakage
\and CICIDS2017 \and Model serving \and Network security.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Learning-based intrusion detection reports extraordinary accuracy on public
benchmarks -- macro-F1 above 0.98 is routine -- and practitioners do not
observe it in the field. The discrepancy is usually attributed to concept
drift or to the age of the corpora. We examine a more immediate contributor:
much of the reported accuracy is an artefact of how the benchmark is split
and of what the reported metric measures.

A random split rewards recognition rather than detection. Flows from one
attack episode are numerous and near-identical, and a random split scatters
them across the train/test boundary, so a model scores well by recognising
near-duplicates it has memorised. That random splits flatter intrusion
detectors has been observed
before~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers,xu2025robust} and we do not claim the
observation; we contribute a protocol that removes the confound and a
measurement of what it costs. Separately, precision depends on the prevalence
of attacks, and benchmarks carry orders of magnitude more attack traffic than
a production link, so a precision measured at a 17.7\,\% attack rate says
little about the alert queue at one in a thousand. A third gap is
engineering: papers routinely support a deployment claim with an
\emph{amortised per-sample model cost}, which omits feature extraction,
parsing, serialisation, transport and concurrency. It is a lower bound on the
cost of the model, not a property of a service. An earlier version of this
work converted such a figure into a throughput, which prompted this study.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Holding models, hyperparameters and preprocessing fixed and varying only the
evaluation, we contribute a pipeline in which scaling, selection and
oversampling are re-fitted \emph{inside} every training split
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}); four splitting protocols on identical data --
repeated holdout, day-disjoint (which here is also family-disjoint),
leave-one-family-out and genuine cross-dataset transfer
(Section~\ref{sec:method}); paired tests over repeated seeds, showing the
commonly reported ranking of the leading models to be unsupported
(Section~\ref{sec:paired}); measured error rates translated into precision
and alert burden at operational prevalence (Section~\ref{sec:operational});
and an end-to-end measurement of the deployed service, reported separately
from model cost (Section~\ref{sec:serving}). The experiment driver, serving
implementation, container and Kubernetes definitions, load profile and raw
results -- from which every table and figure here is generated -- are
released with the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Buczak and Guven~\cite{buczak2016survey} survey the method families; our
concern is evaluation rather than models.

NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nslkdd} corrected redundant records in KDD'99,
UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015unswnb15} introduced a contemporary feature set,
and CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} added five days of labelled
traffic across several attack families. CICIDS2017 is not a neutral
instrument: Engelen et al.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting} document
labelling and capture defects -- misordered and duplicated packets, executed
but unlabelled attacks -- and Lanvin et al.~\cite{lanvin2023errors} show that
correcting them changes measured performance materially. We therefore report
differences between protocols on identical data rather than absolute scores.

That random splits flatter intrusion detectors is not new and we do not claim
it. Moczkodan and Ragab~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers} compare random,
temporal and five-tuple-grouped splits on this corpus and find the random
convention substantially overestimates performance; Xu and
Liu~\cite{xu2025robust} report severe recall loss on novel attacks. We extend
the observation along three axes they leave open: a day-disjoint split, which
here is simultaneously family-disjoint; a translation of error rates into
precision at operational prevalence; and a measurement of serving cost. That
last is treated most loosely in the literature, where an amortised per-sample
model cost -- typically of order $10^{-2}$~ms -- is offered as evidence of
deployability although it excludes parsing, serialisation, transport,
queueing and concurrency. Table~\ref{tab:related} summarises the positioning;
recent applied studies~\cite{talukder2024ml,almuhanna2025dl} confirm the
benchmark's continued use.

\section{Data and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}
CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} holds five working days of labelled
flows -- benign traffic plus denial-of-service, DDoS, brute-force,
web-attack, botnet, port-scan and infiltration families -- each described by
more than eighty flow statistics, in eight CSV files, one per capture period.
We draw a fixed 20\,\% stratified sample from each. After normalising column
names, replacing infinities, dropping incomplete records and removing exact
duplicates, the working set holds 535{,}069 flows with 78 numeric features
and an attack ratio of 0.1769.

Each flow keeps its \emph{capture day}, recovered from its source file, and
its \emph{original multi-class label}: the first makes the day-disjoint split
possible, the second the per-family analysis. The binary target maps every
non-benign label to the positive class. Standardisation, top-$k$ selection by
Random Forest importance~\cite{breiman2001random} and oversampling with
SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote} are treated as part of the model, not of the
data (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:method}
The models in this study are deliberately ordinary. What varies, and what the
paper is about, is the protocol under which they are evaluated. This section
fixes that protocol precisely enough to be re-run.

\subsection{Models, Hyperparameters and Leakage Control}
\label{sec:leakage}
We compare Logistic Regression as a linear baseline, Random
Forest~\cite{breiman2001random}, XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} and a
two-hidden-layer multilayer perceptron. Every hyperparameter is fixed a
priori and identical across every split, seed and dataset, and we perform no
tuning. Logistic Regression uses an $\ell_2$ penalty with $C=1$, the
\texttt{lbfgs} solver and at most 1000 iterations. Random Forest uses 200
trees, unlimited depth, minimum split size 2, minimum leaf size 1,
$\sqrt{d}$ features per split and the Gini criterion. XGBoost uses 300
rounds, maximum depth 8, learning rate 0.3, no subsampling of rows or
columns, $\lambda=1$ and the \texttt{hist} tree method. The perceptron uses
hidden layers $(128,64)$, ReLU activations, Adam with $\alpha=10^{-4}$,
batch size 200, initial learning rate $10^{-3}$, and at most 50 epochs with
early stopping. The complete machine-readable specification, exported from
the objects the experiments instantiate, is released with the artefact.

Absolute scores are therefore not the best attainable, but no model is
advantaged by a larger search budget and no hyperparameter can have been
selected on test data. Since the paper reports differences between protocols
on a fixed pipeline, this removes a confound rather than weakening the
result.


\subsection{Four Splitting Protocols}
The same pipeline is evaluated under four separations of training from test.
Reporting them side by side is the point: only the last two resemble what a
deployed sensor faces.

\textbf{(P1) Repeated stratified holdout} -- a 70/30 split over five seeds,
the conventional protocol and our reference point. One split cannot separate
a real difference between models from split-to-split noise.

\textbf{(P2) Day-disjoint} -- training on Monday--Wednesday, testing on
Thursday--Friday, so no capture day appears on both sides and the
near-duplicate flows a random split scatters across the boundary are removed.
Each attack family on CICIDS2017 was executed on one specific day, so this
split is necessarily also \emph{family-disjoint}: every attack at test time is
of a type absent from training.

\textbf{(P3) Leave-one-family-out} -- P2 confounds the day boundary with the
family boundary; P3 separates them by removing one family from training
entirely and asking the model to flag it, family by family.

\textbf{(P4) Cross-dataset transfer} -- fitted on one corpus, evaluated on
the other with no target-domain data. The corpora share no schema, so
transfer requires an explicit alignment (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). We
distinguish this from \emph{independent-dataset replication}, in which the
protocol is re-run within UNSW-NB15 to check whether the model ordering
reproduces; the two are frequently conflated, including in an earlier version
of this work, and only P4 speaks to cross-domain behaviour.

\subsection{Statistical Treatment and Metrics}
\label{sec:stats}
Every protocol that admits repetition is repeated over multiple seeds, with
all models evaluated on identical splits, so comparisons are paired. We
report means with the half-width of the 95\,\% confidence interval, and for
the leading pair a paired $t$-test and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the
shared seeds, so that a claim of superiority rests on a test rather than on
a difference in the fourth decimal place.

Alongside accuracy, precision, recall and macro-F1 we report the false
positive and false negative rates, the Matthews correlation coefficient,
balanced accuracy, and the area under both the ROC and the
precision--recall curve; PR-AUC is the more informative of the two under
heavy imbalance, since ROC-AUC is insensitive to the absolute number of
false positives when negatives dominate.

\paragraph{Operational prevalence.}
Precision depends on prevalence while TPR and FPR do not, so a precision
measured on a benchmark does not transfer to deployment. For prevalence
$\pi$, the precision implied by a measured $(\mathrm{TPR}, \mathrm{FPR})$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ppv}
\mathrm{PPV}(\pi)=\frac{\pi\cdot\mathrm{TPR}}
{\pi\cdot\mathrm{TPR}+(1-\pi)\cdot\mathrm{FPR}},
\end{equation}
which we evaluate at $\pi \in \{10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\}$ together
with the number of false alerts per million flows -- the quantity that
decides whether a detector is usable at all.

\subsection{Cost Measurement}
Training time and inference time are measured on the environment of
Section~\ref{sec:cost}. Inference is timed after a discarded warm-up pass, as
the mean of five repeats, on the full test batch with all cores available.
The resulting per-sample figure is an amortised lower bound on model cost.
We do not convert it into a service throughput. What a service actually
sustains is measured separately, on the running endpoint, in
Section~\ref{sec:serving}.

\subsection{Is the Leading Model Actually Leading?}
\label{sec:paired}
The usual conclusion from a table like this is that XGBoost wins.
A paired test over the shared seeds says otherwise. XGBoost does lead Random Forest, but by 0.00044 macro-F1, and
neither the paired $t$-test ($p=0.13$) nor the Wilcoxon signed-rank test
($p=0.19$) separates that from zero. Both ensembles are separated from
Logistic Regression and the MLP by margins that are large and clearly
significant.

We therefore do not name a best model. What the data supports is two tiers --
tree ensembles above the linear and shallow neural baselines -- with the
choice inside the upper tier resting on other grounds, which
Section~\ref{sec:cost} shows are not negligible.

\subsection{P2: Day-Disjoint, and the Optimism of a Random Split}
\label{sec:p2}
Training uses Monday--Wednesday and testing Thursday--Friday, and the two
sides share no attack family: training holds the denial-of-service variants,
both brute-force families and Heartbleed, testing holds botnet, DDoS,
infiltration, port scanning and the three web-attack variants.

The effect is not a third-decimal correction. Attack recall for XGBoost falls
from 0.9935 to 0.3164 and for Random Forest from 0.9937 to 0.3109, macro-F1
falling by roughly 0.30 for both: models that find essentially every attack
under the conventional protocol miss two in three under this one.
Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows where the errors go, and the direction
matters. The false-positive rate does not deteriorate -- it improves, from
0.0073 to 0.0030 -- while false negatives rise from 185 flows to 36{,}506.
The detector does not become noisy; it becomes quiet, confident and wrong,
the failure a monitoring dashboard is least likely to reveal.

Nor does the ranking survive. Logistic Regression is last under the random
split (macro-F1 0.8515 against 0.9879) and first under the day-disjoint one
(0.7433 against 0.6887), with attack recall 0.4975 against 0.3164. The gap is
larger for the models that score best, so the conventional protocol can
recommend the wrong model, not merely inflate a number;
Section~\ref{sec:operational} qualifies what that inversion does and does not
imply. One caveat bounds the comparison: the day boundary removes
near-duplicate flows and attack families at once, and the next section
separates them.

\subsection{P3: Per-Family Behaviour and Unseen Attack Types}
\label{sec:p3}
Aggregate scores average over families that behave very differently, weighted
by flow count. Two things stand out in Figure~\ref{fig:family}. A macro-F1 of
0.988 coexists with web-attack recalls near zero -- on the same split,
XGBoost detects SQL injection at 0.000 and cross-site scripting at 0.023.
These families are a few hundred flows each, so they cost the aggregate
almost nothing, and they are not the families a defender would choose to
miss. And the model that looks worst in aggregate is the only one that
detects them: Logistic Regression reaches 1.00 on XSS and 0.93 on web brute
force where the three stronger models sit between 0.02 and 0.09. The
mechanism is its order-of-magnitude higher false-positive rate; whether that
is a virtue depends on the alert budget, which
Section~\ref{sec:operational} makes quantitative.

\resfig{fig_per_family}{Recall by attack family under the random split. The
model with the weakest aggregate score is the only one that detects the
web-attack families at all.}{fig:family}

Leave-one-family-out removes a family from training entirely, then asks the
detector to flag it, and the outcome divides rather than
grades. Where a sibling variant remains, generalisation holds: holding out
DoS~Hulk or DoS~slowloris still yields recall near 0.88. Where the family
stands alone it collapses: botnet traffic is detected at 0.000 and port
scanning at 0.0036 across 26{,}822 held-out flows. A supervised flow classifier of this kind interpolates within a family and
does not extrapolate to a new one; a detector missing 99.6\,\% of port
scanning when it was absent from training is not performing anomaly detection
in any useful sense, whatever its benchmark score.

\subsection{Feature Selection: Reproducible but Not Canonical}
\label{sec:featstab}
Across seeds the selection is highly reproducible -- mean pairwise Jaccard
0.756, with 15 of 20 features chosen by every seed. Across \emph{criteria} it
is not: impurity and permutation importance, computed on the same split with
the same forest, agree on only 4 of their top 20. Impurity favours
packet-length statistics; permutation puts destination port, forward
inter-arrival minimum and initial TCP window sizes first; mutual information
agrees closely with impurity (0.75) and barely with permutation (0.25). The
divergence is the known bias of impurity importance towards high-cardinality
correlated continuous features, and no aggregate score would reveal it, since
either list yields a similar macro-F1. A published top-$k$ list is therefore
a property of the criterion as much as of the traffic.

\subsection{Independent Replication, and P4: Cross-Dataset Transfer}
\label{sec:transfer}
Two experiments are commonly conflated under the heading of cross-dataset
evaluation, as an earlier version of this work conflated them. They answer
different questions.

\emph{Independent-dataset replication} re-runs the whole protocol
\emph{within} UNSW-NB15 -- same pipeline, same hyperparameters, different
corpus. There XGBoost reaches macro-F1 0.9504 and Random Forest 0.9489, so
the two-tier ordering reproduces on independent data. It checks whether a pattern is an artefact of
one benchmark. It is not evidence that a detector fitted on one corpus works
on another, since no model leaves its own dataset.

\emph{Transfer} fits on one corpus and evaluates on the other with no
target-domain data. The datasets share no schema, so we align the seven
flow-level quantities whose semantics match -- duration, forward and backward
packet counts, byte counts and mean packet sizes -- rescaling CICIDS2017
duration from microseconds to seconds. That restriction is itself a confound,
so we measure it: an ordinary in-domain holdout on exactly these seven
features gives XGBoost macro-F1 0.9911 on CICIDS2017 and 0.9289 on UNSW-NB15,
against 0.9879 and 0.9504 on twenty features. The reduced feature space costs
almost nothing in-domain, so what follows is domain shift, not feature
starvation.

Transfer fails in both directions, and differently in each
below. Fitted on CICIDS2017 and tested on UNSW-NB15,
the models call almost everything benign: attack recall is 0.0000 for Random
Forest, 0.0001 for XGBoost, 0.0229 for the MLP. Fitted on UNSW-NB15 and
tested on CICIDS2017 they do the opposite, with false-positive rates from
0.55 to 0.89 and precision between 0.11 and 0.21 despite recall as high as
0.85. Each failure runs towards the majority decision of its source corpus --
CICIDS2017 is 17.7\,\% attack, UNSW-NB15 68.1\,\% -- so what transfers is a
threshold calibrated to one dataset's prevalence and scaling, not a notion of
malicious traffic. Macro-F1 lands between 0.24 and 0.42 against 0.99
in-domain: none of these transferred models is usable.

The conclusion is deliberately narrow. A flow-statistics classifier of this
family, trained on one public corpus, does not carry to another, which
contradicts the practice of citing an in-domain score as evidence of general
applicability. It does not show cross-domain detection to be impossible;
domain adaptation, recalibration on target traffic, and features chosen for
invariance are untested here and are the obvious next step.

\subsection{What the Error Rates Mean in Operation}
\label{sec:operational}
Every number so far was measured on a corpus that is 17.7\,\% attack; a
production link is not. Because precision depends on prevalence while the
error rates do not, the measured rates carry to a realistic prevalence
through Equation~\ref{eq:ppv} with no further experiment
(Table~\ref{tab:operational}).

All four models collapse, and the collapse is governed by the false-positive
rate rather than the recall the benchmark tables emphasise. Take the most
favourable case in this paper: XGBoost under the random split, recall 0.9935,
false-positive rate 0.0073. At one attack in a thousand its precision is
0.120 -- seven of every eight alerts false -- and it raises 7{,}257 false
alerts per million flows. At one in ten thousand, precision is 0.013.

\inputtable{operational}

Comparing protocols in that table produces a result easy to misread. Moving
to the day-disjoint protocol, the alert burden of XGBoost \emph{improves}:
false alerts per million fall from 7{,}257 to 3{,}006 and precision at one in
a thousand barely moves, 0.120 to 0.095, while recall falls from 0.9935 to
0.3164. An operator watching alert volume would see a system that had got
quieter and no less accurate. The degradation is invisible in exactly the
signals a deployment has.

The inversion of Section~\ref{sec:p2} does not, however, recommend Logistic
Regression. Its false-positive rate is an order of magnitude above the
ensembles', so at one-in-a-thousand prevalence its precision is 0.0083 and it
raises 59{,}143 false alerts per million -- twenty times the burden of
XGBoost for a recall advantage of 0.18. The conclusion is narrower: neither
the conventional protocol nor macro-F1 can be trusted to rank detectors once
prevalence is accounted for. The UNSW-NB15 replication sharpens it: the
ensembles reach recall above 0.96 there at a false-positive rate near 0.075,
so precision at one in a thousand is 0.013 rather than 0.120 -- an order of
magnitude of operational value separating two standard benchmarks.

\subsection{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:cost}
Timings were taken after a discarded warm-up pass, as the mean of five
repeats, on a 16-core AMD workstation with 27.8~GiB of memory running
Python 3.13, scikit-learn 1.5.2, XGBoost 3.4.1 and imbalanced-learn 0.14.2.
XGBoost trains in $5.96 \pm 1.41$~s against $32.65 \pm 2.48$~s for Random Forest
-- roughly a sixth of the cost for a difference in quality that
Section~\ref{sec:paired} could not distinguish from zero. Since no accuracy
grounds separate them, cost is a legitimate basis for the choice, and it is
the only one this paper found.

Amortised inference is 0.001056~ms per flow for XGBoost. That is model cost
spread over a full batch on all cores: a lower bound on the cost of serving a
flow, which we do not convert into a throughput. What a service sustains is
measured in Section~\ref{sec:serving}.

\section{Deployment and Measured Serving Cost}
\label{sec:serving}
An offline score says what a detector would decide; it does not say what the
system around it costs to run. This section closes that gap by measuring the
deployed service rather than describing it.

\subsection{The Service and the Load Protocol}
The selected model is exported with the per-column standardisation statistics
of its features and wrapped in a stateless FastAPI service exposing liveness,
readiness (failing until the model is resident), metadata, and single-flow
and batch scoring endpoints. Every response carries the in-process scoring
time, letting the client separate model cost from queueing, parsing and
transport. The image is pinned, runs as a non-root user, and runs one worker
per replica so pod-level scaling is not confounded by in-process
parallelism. The artefact served is the artefact evaluated -- same pipeline,
same training split, same leakage controls.

Load is closed-loop: each of $N$ clients issues its next request as the
previous response returns, the behaviour of a bounded worker pool, which
unlike an open-loop rate test cannot mask saturation behind an unbounded
queue. Each level begins with a discarded warm-up, payloads are held-out
CICIDS2017 flows replayed in sequence, and we report the distribution from
median to 99.9th percentile because a mean conceals the tail that decides
whether an inline sensor is usable.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:servingresults}
A single replica sustains 309~requests/s at eight concurrent clients, at a
median of 24~ms and a 95th percentile of 39~ms, with the numerical libraries
left at their default thread setting. Beyond that, throughput does
not merely stop rising but falls: 188~requests/s at 32 clients with a 95th
percentile of 578~ms, and 128~requests/s at 128 clients with a 99th
percentile of 6.2~s. No request failed at any level. This is ordinary congestion collapse for a single-worker service, and it is
why we report a distribution and a saturation point rather than a peak
rate.

The amortised model cost of Section~\ref{sec:cost}, 0.001056~ms per flow,
inverts to about $9.5\times10^{5}$~flows/s. The best configuration we
measured sustains 404~requests/s on the single-flow path
(Section~\ref{sec:replication}) -- an overstatement by a factor of roughly
2{,}300, and the sweep above, run at the library's default thread setting,
is slower still. Batching recovers much but not all of the gap: at a
batch of 256 the same replica processes 34{,}662~flows/s, still 27 times
below the extrapolation, at a 95th-percentile request latency of 88~ms. The
residue is what the amortised figure omits -- HTTP parsing, JSON
deserialisation, per-request interpreter overhead, scheduling and queueing.
An earlier version of this work quoted $10^{5}$~predictions/s on exactly that
basis; no care taken in the offline evaluation would have caught the error,
only running the service does. Batching is thus a deployment policy with a measurable price on each side
-- 114 times the flow throughput for twice the median request latency --
invisible in any offline model comparison.

From process launch to the first successful readiness response the service
takes 7.83~s, of which 1.95~s is loading and warming the model; this bounds
how fast capacity can be added, excluding the scheduling and image-pull
components a cluster would add.

\subsection{Replication, Failure and the Resource Envelope}
\label{sec:replication}
Three properties usually deferred to a cluster need no scheduler, only more
than one replica and the willingness to kill one. We run $N$ independent
service processes, round-robin the load across them at eight clients per
replica, kill one under load, and record CPU and memory throughout.

\inputtable{scaling}
\resfig{fig_scaling}{Replica scaling on a single host. Whether replication
adds capacity at all is decided by one environment variable: with the maths
libraries left free to use every core, throughput \emph{falls} as replicas
are added.}{fig:scaling}

\paragraph{One environment variable decides whether replication works.}
Each replica scores a single 20-feature row per request, and by default the
numerical libraries fan that work across every core, so replicas contend for
the same sixteen: throughput \emph{falls} monotonically from 269 to
88~requests/s as replicas go from one to eight. Restricting each replica to
one maths thread -- what the container image sets, and what we had previously
asserted without evidence -- is worth between 1.5 and 2.8 times the
throughput at every replica count, and 404 against 269~requests/s even for a
single replica, where no contention between replicas exists. The fan-out
costs more than it saves at this problem size. Past two replicas throughput
declines even when pinned, but the load generator is a Python process on the
same cores, so that portion of the curve is bounded by client contention as
well as by the service; the thread setting is the part that transfers to any
host.

\paragraph{Losing a replica costs 35 requests.}
With four replicas under load we killed one outright and let a supervisor
restart it, as a Deployment would, repeating the injection three times. Every
run lost exactly 35 requests of roughly 8{,}000, the replica was serving
again after 3.13~s, and aggregate throughput returned to its pre-failure
level 8~s after the kill. A Kubernetes Service would also remove the dead
endpoint from rotation, which this client does not, so 35 is an upper bound
on what a cluster would lose.

\paragraph{The resource envelope, measured rather than assumed.}
Resident memory is 176~MiB per replica when pinned, 189--196~MiB when not,
and flat in the number of replicas. Our Deployment requested 512~MiB and
limited at 1~GiB, chosen before any measurement and roughly three and six
times the observed peak; they are now set from this measurement, which is the
discipline the rest of the paper applies to accuracy.

\paragraph{What still requires a cluster.}
Image pull, pod placement and kubelet admission, and the sync delay of the
autoscaler's own control loop are properties of the scheduler rather than of
the service. They are not measured here and we do not estimate them.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
CICIDS2017 was captured in 2017 and carries documented labelling and capture
defects~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting,lanvin2023errors}, so absolute
scores on it are not estimates of field accuracy. The quantities we emphasise
are differences between protocols on identical data, which are far more
robust to label noise than levels, since a defect inflating one protocol
inflates the other; the size of the gap on a corrected or modern corpus is
not established here.

Day-disjoint is not the only leakage-resistant split: grouping by five-tuple
or by timestamp~\cite{moczkodan2026transformers} would separate
near-duplicate flows from unseen families more cleanly. All hyperparameters
are fixed a priori, so absolute scores are not the best each family could
reach; we would expect tuning against a random-split validation set to widen
the gap rather than close it. The operational precision figures follow
exactly from Equation~\ref{eq:ppv} given the measured rates, but assume those
rates transfer to a link with a different traffic mix, which
Section~\ref{sec:transfer} suggests is optimistic; read them as an upper
bound. Finally, the serving measurements are single-host: replica scaling past
four replicas is bounded by the co-resident load generator as well as by the
service, and image pull, pod placement and the autoscaler's control-loop
delay are properties of a scheduler, named as unmeasured rather than
estimated.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On CICIDS2017, with everything but the split held fixed, most of a benchmark
score belongs to the protocol rather than to the detector: attack recall for
the tree ensembles falls from about 0.99 to about 0.31 once no attack family
is shared between training and test, and the ranking inverts, so the
conventional protocol can recommend the wrong model rather than merely
inflate a number. A single split is not a result; nor is an aggregate binary
score, which averages over families whose detectability differs by an order
of magnitude; nor does precision measured at benchmark prevalence survive the
move to a production link, where the degradation is invisible to an operator
watching alert volume. The engineering claim meets the same standard: the
served endpoint sustains 309~requests/s where its amortised model cost would
predict $9.5\times10^{5}$~flows/s, and what we did not measure is named
rather than estimated.

None of this says learned intrusion detection does not work; it says the
published numbers answer an easier question than the operational one. The gap
between protocols estimates that difference, is cheap to compute, and belongs
alongside the conventional figure. Three directions follow: re-run the
protocol on a corrected~\cite{lanvin2023errors} and on a contemporary corpus;
evaluate one-class and anomaly-based detectors, built for unseen families,
under the same day-disjoint protocol, where they may overturn the ordering
reported here; and complete the cluster-level serving measurements, since
autoscaling under attack-driven load spikes is precisely the case an
intrusion detector must survive.
